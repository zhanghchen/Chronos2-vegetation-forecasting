\begin{abstract}
Leaf Area Index (LAI) is strongly seasonal, so raw forecast accuracy at a high-amplitude site can
be driven largely by reconstructing a predictable annual cycle rather than by weather-driven
interannual skill. We ask how much a pretrained time-series foundation model (TSFM), Amazon's
Chronos-2, adds beyond a simple, training-period-only seasonal climatology baseline when applied
zero-shot to LAI hindcasting, and where that addition holds up or breaks down. Using LAI and
meteorological covariates at three U.S.\ vegetation pixels (grassland, deciduous forest, evergreen
forest), we benchmark zero-shot Chronos-2 against this climatology baseline and against eight
supervised models (Random Forest, SVM, five recurrent/convolutional architectures, and the
domain-specific AELSTM model) across a 33-case rolling-window annual hindcast (2012--2022; 330
model--case evaluations across all ten methods), and test spatial generalization and task-specific
adaptation (seven parameter-efficient fine-tuning variants; four vegetation-composition
conditioning architectures, including a randomized-assignment control). Paired across all 33
hindcast cases, Chronos-2's advantage over climatology is not statistically detectable (mean
paired $R^2$ difference $+0.038$, median $+0.006$; Wilcoxon $p=0.242$, $n=33$; the 33 cases are
temporally dependent within only 3 sites, so this is a descriptive rather than a confirmatory
test). Climatology is strictly higher in 15/33 cases, including a higher mean $R^2$ than Chronos-2
at the site with the largest seasonal amplitude (0.964 vs.\ 0.957) -- the same site behind this
study's highest raw $R^2$ values. At the lowest-amplitude site, Chronos-2's paired advantage is
positive and the largest of the three sites; across all 33 cases, its forecast deviation from
climatology is positively correlated with the true deviation more often than not (mean ACC
$0.199$; 25/33 cases positive), though this falls short of an independently validated anomaly-skill
result. Chronos-2 is competitive with the eight supervised baselines under an information regime
that favors it, and a single spatial-transfer test shows no observed degradation relative to a
locally-fit adapter. No adaptation method shows a consistent gain over zero-shot Chronos-2 in the
configurations tested, and a randomized shuffled-assignment control shows a vegetation-composition
conditioning head performs statistically indistinguishably whether trained on true or randomly
permuted compositions (paired $t$-test $p=0.245$, $n=70$) -- though with only one tested
architecture reaching this comparison, we report this as no detected benefit under the tested
protocol rather than an identified mechanism. We read this pattern -- modest, site-dependent skill
over a simple climatology baseline, and no adaptation method shown to improve on it -- as a caution
against treating high raw $R^2$ on strongly seasonal targets as evidence of transferred forecasting
skill, and as a case for climatology-referenced evaluation when applying general-purpose TSFMs to
seasonal Earth-system variables.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Vegetation and LAI forecasting}
\label{sec:intro-lai}

Leaf Area Index -- the one-sided area of leaf tissue per unit ground area -- is a primary control
on canopy photosynthesis, evapotranspiration, and land--atmosphere energy exchange, and is a
standard input to crop-yield, drought, and land-surface models. Forecasting LAI ahead of a
growing season, or under alternative climate scenarios, has historically relied on statistical
regression, tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, \cite{lai_forecasting_TODO}), and increasingly
recurrent or attention-augmented deep-learning architectures trained from scratch per location
\cite{ts_forecasting_survey_TODO}. This project builds directly on one such purpose-built
architecture, AELSTM \cite{aelstm_TODO}, evaluated on the same pixels and covariates used here.

\subsection{The rise of time-series foundation models}
\label{sec:intro-tsfm}

Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) -- large sequence models pretrained on broad, cross-domain
time-series corpora and applied zero-shot to new series -- have recently shown strong generic
forecasting performance without domain-specific training \cite{ansari2024chronos,tsfm_review_TODO}.
Chronos-2 \citep[Amazon, 2025;][]{ansari2025chronos2} is distinguished among these by native,
first-class support for \emph{known future covariates}: rather than only extrapolating a
univariate history, it can condition a forecast on covariates whose future values are already
known -- precisely the structure of a vegetation forecast made with actual (observed, or
scenario) future weather.

\subsection{Research gap}
\label{sec:intro-gap}

TSFMs are extensively benchmarked on generic forecasting suites, but rarely evaluated on
Earth-system or vegetation-specific tasks where (a) covariates carry a known, physically
meaningful future trajectory, (b) target-domain training data is often limited to a handful of
locations and years, (c) the target itself is strongly seasonal, so that raw accuracy conflates
``reconstructing a predictable annual cycle'' with ``forecasting the genuine, weather-driven
deviation from it,'' and (d) the practical question is not ``can this model forecast at all'' but
``does its skill exceed a simple, interpretable seasonal baseline, and does adapting it to this
domain help beyond that.'' Point (c) is rarely addressed directly in TSFM evaluations on seasonal
environmental variables, even though a climatology baseline is cheap to compute and, as this paper
shows, far from trivial to beat. This gap motivates using Chronos-2 as a concrete test case for
vegetation LAI forecasting \cite{earth_system_fm_TODO}.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rq}

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1.] How much does pretrained, zero-shot Chronos-2 improve on a simple, training-period-only
    seasonal climatology baseline, and is that improvement consistent across sites and years?
  \item[RQ2.] How does its performance compare with conventional supervised ML and deep-learning
    baselines trained specifically for this task?
  \item[RQ3.] How robust is Chronos-2 across different years, vegetation regimes, and locations,
    and does its robustness (or lack of it) track its climatology-referenced skill?
  \item[RQ4.] Under what conditions does it perform particularly well or poorly?
  \item[RQ5.] Does task-specific parameter adaptation improve Chronos-2 beyond its pretrained
    zero-shot capability -- and beyond the climatology baseline?
  \item[RQ6.] What do these experiments imply for applying general-purpose TSFMs to seasonal
    Earth-system forecasting, and for how such claims should be evaluated?
\end{description}

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We introduce a climatology-referenced evaluation of zero-shot Chronos-2 for vegetation LAI
    forecasting -- a training-period-only seasonal climatology baseline, computed with the same
    no-leakage protocol across all 33 rolling-window annual hindcast cases (3 pixels $\times$ 11
    years) -- and show that a large share of the raw accuracy reported in single-year TSFM
    evaluations on seasonal targets is attributable to seasonal-cycle reconstruction rather than
    genuine interannual skill: paired across all 33 cases, Chronos-2's advantage over climatology
    is not statistically detectable, and climatology is strictly higher at the site responsible
    for the highest raw $R^2$ in this study.
  \item We benchmark zero-shot Chronos-2 against eight supervised baselines (raw-observation
    rescoring) across three vegetation regimes and 330 model--case evaluations (33 rolling-window
    hindcast cases $\times$ 10 methods),
    and characterize its robustness and generalization behavior -- temporal (across 11 years),
    spatial (a 3{,}700\,km transfer test), and failure-mode-specific (a controlled leakage
    diagnostic isolating distribution shift from representational limitation) -- interpreting all
    of these results jointly with the climatology comparison rather than in isolation.
  \item We conduct the most extensive adaptation study of Chronos-2 for a domain task to date
    within this project -- seven parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods and four
    vegetation-composition conditioning architectures, including a randomized shuffled-assignment
    control -- and show that none shows a consistent gain over the pretrained zero-shot
    representation, which itself does not consistently exceed climatology; the shuffle control
    rules out ``wrong information, right mechanism'' as an explanation for the one architecture
    that trains successfully, though we stop short of identifying the underlying mechanism and
    report this as a candidate hypothesis in the Discussion rather than a settled finding.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Forecasting formulation}
\label{sec:data-formulation}

For the primary task, Chronos-2 is given historical LAI (2000--2021, $\sim$1{,}002 8-day steps) as
\texttt{target}, the same-period climate as \texttt{past\_covariates}, and the \emph{actual
observed} 2022 climate as \texttt{future\_covariates}, forecasting all 45 steps of 2022 in a
single direct (non-autoregressive) call -- well within Chronos-2's native 1{,}024-step
direct-forecasting horizon. This is a deliberately different task from the AELSTM baselines,
which predict LAI from past climate alone; the implications of this asymmetry are addressed
directly in \Cref{sec:results-comparison} and \Cref{sec:limitations}. We extend this to 11
held-out years (2012--2022) using a \textbf{fixed 12-year rolling training window} immediately
preceding each held-out year, which we call a \textbf{rolling-window annual hindcast} rather than
leave-one-year-out cross-validation (LOYO-CV) throughout this paper: it is not classic
all-other-years LOYO, which would leak future years into a forecaster, and not an expanding
window, which would confound ``difficult year'' with ``how much training data this fold happened
to receive'').

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Chronos-2}
\label{sec:methods-chronos2}

Chronos-2 is a pretrained, T5-style encoder-only patch-transformer (12 blocks, 119.5M parameters)
that stacks a target series and its covariates into a shared representation, normalizes each
series independently by its own per-series statistics (InstanceNorm) before patch-embedding, and
produces a direct multi-step forecast. For a series $x = (x_1, \ldots, x_T)$, the normalization is

\begin{align}
  \mathrm{loc} &= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} x_t, \label{eq:in-loc} \\
  \mathrm{scale} &= \begin{cases} \varepsilon & \text{if } \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \mathrm{loc})^2} = 0 \\[4pt] \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \mathrm{loc})^2} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-5}, \label{eq:in-scale} \\
  \tilde{x}_t &= \mathrm{arcsinh}\!\left(\frac{x_t - \mathrm{loc}}{\mathrm{scale}}\right). \label{eq:in-scaled}
\end{align}
We verified \Cref{eq:in-loc}--\Cref{eq:in-scaled} directly against the installed package's
\texttt{InstanceNorm} implementation and the specific \texttt{amazon/chronos-2} checkpoint
configuration used throughout this paper, which sets \texttt{use\_arcsinh=True}; the arcsinh step
is applied after standardization and is part of the actual forward pass, not an optional variant we
chose to describe.

This normalization detail is not incidental -- it is the direct mechanistic cause of one of this
paper's architectural findings (\Cref{sec:results-adaptation}, \Cref{sec:results-failure}): a
covariate that is \emph{exactly constant} across the context and forecast window has
$\mathrm{scale} = \varepsilon$ by the first case of \Cref{eq:in-scale}, so
$\tilde{x}_t = \mathrm{arcsinh}((c - c)/\varepsilon) = \mathrm{arcsinh}(0) = 0$ for every timestep
$t$, regardless of the constant value $c$ -- erasing the covariate's value to exactly zero
regardless of its content. (A covariate that is merely \emph{near}-constant, rather than exactly
so, is not erased this way and instead receives a very large scale factor; we did not test this
intermediate regime.)

For Chronos-2 specifically -- not for the supervised baselines, each of which produces a
conventional single-valued point prediction with no associated quantile output -- the model's
native interface returns a set of forecast quantiles together with the predictive mean, and every
Chronos-2 point forecast reported in this paper is this predictive \textbf{mean}
(\texttt{pipeline.predict\_quantiles(...)}'s second return value, queried at quantile levels
$\{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}$ for the quantile outputs themselves, which are not otherwise used in this
paper's point-forecast evaluation), not the $0.5$-quantile/median output; we confirmed this
directly against the calling code used to produce every Chronos-2 result in this paper. Negative
Chronos-2 predictions are not clipped to the physically valid $\mathrm{LAI} \geq 0$ range before
scoring.

\subsection{Zero-shot forecasting setup}
\label{sec:methods-zeroshot}

No trainable weights; the pretrained checkpoint is applied directly with the input structure of
\Cref{sec:data-formulation}. Kept identical across every experiment in this paper as the
reference condition.

\subsection{Baseline models}
\label{sec:methods-baselines}

Eight models from the sibling AELSTM project, each trained from scratch per pixel on
\textbf{past climate only}: Random Forest, SVM, and five neural sequence architectures (RNN,
LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, CNN), plus AELSTM itself, an attention-augmented LSTM architecture purpose-built
for this task. We confirmed directly against the baseline pipeline's own sequence-construction code
that this excludes \emph{both} future climate \emph{and} the target's own lagged history: the input
window passed to every baseline is the 7 climate covariates only (column-sliced to exclude the LAI
column before windowing), so these models never see past LAI, let alone future climate, as an
input feature -- a starker information asymmetry with Chronos-2 (\Cref{sec:results-comparison})
than ``past vs.\ future climate'' alone would suggest. The baseline pipeline also applies a
Savitzky--Golay smoothing filter to both the climate inputs and the LAI target before training; all
eight models are rescored here against the same raw, unsmoothed LAI observations Chronos-2 is
evaluated against, correcting the target-side mismatch, but the climate-input smoothing itself is
not undone and remains a further, smaller difference in what information each model family
receives (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Parameter adaptation methods}
\label{sec:methods-adaptation}

\textbf{LoRA} \citep{hu2022lora} (original: $\mathit{lr}=10^{-4}$, rank 8/$\alpha$16, fixed 1{,}000
steps, no validation; improved: chronological validation, 6-config search over
$\mathit{lr}\in\{10^{-5},10^{-4}\}\times\mathrm{rank}\in\{4,8,16\}$, early stopping, two-stage
refit).

\textbf{Six additional PEFT methods} (DoRA~\citep{liu2024dora}, VeRA~\citep{kopiczko2024vera},
IA3~\citep{liu2022ia3}, LN-Tuning~\citep{arxiv2409.11302}, BitFit~\citep{zaken2022bitfit}, and
partial last-transformer-block fine-tuning), implemented via a generalized fit wrapper that
replicates Chronos-2's public \texttt{fit()} internals but accepts any \texttt{peft.PeftConfig},
under an identical validated protocol and an equal-sized 4-config search budget per method.

\textbf{PFT-composition conditioning}: a FiLM-style~\citep{perez2018film} side-input head
(vegetation-composition encoder $\rightarrow$ feature-wise affine modulation of the target row's
forecast hidden states, base model frozen),
\begin{equation}
  h'_{\text{target}} = \gamma \odot h_{\text{target}} + \beta, \qquad (\gamma, \beta) = g_\theta(\mathrm{PFT}),
  \label{eq:film}
\end{equation}
tested in four architecturally distinct forms: a deep MLP head (\texttt{deep\_mlp},
100{,}544 params), a regularized/smaller variant (\texttt{deep\_mlp\_reg}), a
biologically-structured linear mixture of per-class response vectors
(\texttt{linear\_mixture}, motivated by soft mixture-of-experts framings of heterogeneous
remote-sensing data~\citep{stf_moe_pmc12318938} and by contextual/static-feature conditioning
surveys for time-series forecasters~\citep{arxiv2410.12672}),
\begin{equation}
  \gamma = \sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c\, \gamma_c, \qquad \beta = \sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c\, \beta_c,
  \label{eq:linear-mixture}
\end{equation}
where $p_c$ is the fractional cover of PFT class $c$ and $(\gamma_c, \beta_c)$ is a directly
learned, per-class modulation vector; and a rank-8-bottleneck variant (\texttt{low\_rank}) --
plus the randomized-control condition of training the same architecture on randomly shuffled
PFT-to-pixel assignments.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:methods-metrics}

For ground truth $y_i$ and prediction $\hat{y}_i$ over $N$ forecast steps,
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}, \qquad
  \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_i - \hat{y}_i|, \\
  R^2 &= 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}(y_i - \bar{y})^2}, \qquad
  r = \frac{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)(\hat y_i - \bar{\hat y})}{\sqrt{\sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}\sqrt{\sum_i (\hat y_i - \bar{\hat y})^2}}, \\
  \mathrm{MAPE} &= \frac{100\%}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} \left|\frac{y_i - \hat{y}_i}{y_i}\right|,
  \qquad \mathcal{P} = \{\, i : y_i > 0 \ \wedge\ \hat{y}_i > 0 \,\}. \label{eq:mape}
\end{align}
\Cref{eq:mape} matches the AELSTM project's own \texttt{Cal\_mape} convention (percent error
computed only over rows where both the true and predicted values are positive) so that MAPE is
directly comparable across every method in this paper. The rolling-window hindcast additionally reports an anomaly
correlation coefficient (ACC) against a per-fold day-of-year climatology built only from that
fold's own training years.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:expdesign}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Single-split evaluation and fairness}
\label{sec:expdesign-single}

Every baseline is rescored against raw (unsmoothed) LAI observations before any cross-model
comparison; the naive, smoothed-target comparison is retained in the source repository only to
show what the baseline project's own pipeline reports internally, and is explicitly excluded from
this paper's evidence.

\subsection{Rolling-window annual hindcast design}
\label{sec:expdesign-loyo}

330 fold results (3 pixels $\times$ 11 years $\times$ 10 methods), each scored against raw
observed LAI, using the fixed rolling-window design of \Cref{sec:data-formulation}.

\subsection{Spatial-transfer design}
\label{sec:expdesign-spatial}

A single LoRA adapter, fit once on \texttt{evergreen}'s full 2000--2022 series, is evaluated
\textbf{unmodified} at \texttt{evergreen\_west} using that pixel's own context and real 2022
climate -- a legitimate generalization test (the target pixel's data never participates in
fitting), contrasted with the same adapter's performance at its own source pixel and with a
separately-fit target-local adapter.

\subsection{Adaptation search protocols}
\label{sec:expdesign-search}

Every adaptation method in this paper (LoRA-improved, all 6 additional PEFT methods, all 4
PFT-conditioning architectures) uses the same discipline: model/hyperparameter/checkpoint
selection is driven \textbf{only} by a chronological validation split carved from pre-2022 data;
the 2022 test year is evaluated exactly once, after every selection decision is locked in.

\subsection{The PFT shuffled-assignment control}
\label{sec:expdesign-shuffle}

The PFT randomized-assignment control experiment retrains the best-performing conditioning architecture
(\texttt{low\_rank}) identically, except with vegetation-composition vectors randomly permuted
across the 70 pixels before training -- breaking the true pixel$\leftrightarrow$composition link
while preserving the marginal distribution of PFT values the model sees. If real PFT provides
genuine information, the real condition should measurably outperform the shuffled one on the
same, otherwise-untouched validation and test protocol; if not, this is a randomized negative
control, not merely an additional ablation arm.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Benchmarking against seasonal climatology}
\label{sec:results-climatology}

Before comparing Chronos-2 with other forecasting models, we ask the more basic question this
paper is organized around: how much of its accuracy is attributable to reconstructing a
predictable seasonal cycle, versus genuine interannual forecasting skill? We compute a
training-period-only day-of-year climatology for each of the 33 rolling-window annual hindcast
cases (\Cref{sec:expdesign-loyo}; 3 pixels $\times$ 11 test years, not classic leave-one-year-out),
using exactly the same 12-year rolling window and the same no-leakage protocol already used
elsewhere in this paper for the ACC metric (\Cref{sec:methods-metrics}; alignment, missing-value,
and end-of-year handling are specified in that section), and score it directly as a standalone
forecast -- no model, no fitting, just the mean LAI at each calendar position across the fold's own
training years.

\Cref{tab:climatology} reports the result, paired across all 33 cases. \textbf{Chronos-2 shows no
statistically detectable advantage over this simple baseline}: the mean paired $R^2$ difference is
$+0.038$ and the median paired difference is $+0.006$ -- both positive, i.e.\ Chronos-2 is ahead on
average by both measures of the \emph{paired} difference (paired $t$-test $p=0.378$, Wilcoxon
signed-rank $p=0.242$, $n=33$). This should not be confused with the two methods' separate,
unpaired $R^2$ distributions, where climatology's pooled median (0.683) exceeds Chronos-2's (0.644)
-- the two statistics answer different questions (typical paired advantage vs.\ typical absolute
level) and are not required to agree. Climatology is strictly higher on $R^2$ in 15 of 33 cases
(45\%) and Chronos-2 is strictly higher in the remaining 18 (55\%); no case ties exactly. Most
strikingly, at \texttt{high\_amplitude\_deciduous}, the site responsible for this paper's highest
raw $R^2$ numbers (\Cref{sec:results-zeroshot}), climatology has the \emph{higher} mean $R^2$ of the
two (0.964 vs.\ 0.957; mean paired difference $-0.007$, median $-0.010$) and a negative mean MSE
skill score for Chronos-2 ($-0.264$): most of that site's apparent forecasting accuracy is
attributable to its strong, repeatable seasonal cycle, which a three-line climatology computation
reconstructs as well as or better than Chronos-2 does. At \texttt{low\_amplitude}, the pattern
reverses (mean paired difference $+0.115$, median $+0.056$; mean skill score $+0.051$; Chronos-2
mean $R^2$ 0.317 vs.\ climatology's 0.202); \texttt{evergreen} is close to even (mean paired
difference $+0.007$, median $+0.042$). We report these per-site figures explicitly, rather than
only the pooled $p$-value, because the 33 cases are not 33 independent trials: each site
contributes 11 temporally adjacent, overlapping-training-window years, so the pooled test's
effective sample size is closer to 3 (sites) than 33, and the pooled $p$-values above should be
read as descriptive and exploratory rather than as a confirmatory test with 33 independent
replicates.

The anomaly correlation coefficient (ACC) -- the correlation, within each $\sim$45-step test year,
between Chronos-2's forecast deviation from climatology and the true deviation -- gives a
complementary within-site view, computed here across all 33 cases rather than only the single 2022
holdout emphasized in \Cref{sec:results-zeroshot}: positive in 25 of 33 cases (mean $0.199$, median
$0.252$), negative in the remaining 8. We read this as evidence of \emph{some} deseasonalized
anomaly correlation more often than not, not as an established, validated ``genuine interannual
skill'' -- ACC does not test whether the predicted anomaly's \emph{magnitude} is accurate, does not
rule out residual within-year seasonal-phase error contributing to the correlation, and the
case-level sign pattern has not itself been tested for statistical significance. A direct anomaly
error-skill score (deseasonalized RMSE relative to climatology, case by case) would be a stronger
complement to ACC than we compute here and is a natural next step (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

We draw two conclusions from this comparison and keep them separate throughout the remainder of
this paper. First, high raw $R^2$ at a strongly seasonal site is not, by itself, evidence of skill
beyond seasonal-cycle reconstruction -- at \texttt{high\_amplitude\_deciduous} a trivial baseline
matches or exceeds it. Second, this does not generalize to ``Chronos-2 adds nothing'': at
\texttt{low\_amplitude} the paired advantage is real and the largest of the three sites, and the
ACC pattern is majority-positive -- we report this as an observed average margin at this site,
without attributing it to meteorological conditioning specifically, since this study does not run
the with/without-future-covariates ablation that would be needed to isolate that cause from
historical-LAI use, model capacity, or other differences between Chronos-2 and the climatology
baseline.

\subsection{Can parameter adaptation improve Chronos-2?}
\label{sec:results-adaptation}

\textbf{In the configurations tested here, no adaptation method beats zero-shot Chronos-2 on the 3
core pixels' 2022 holdout.} This evidence base is narrower than ``adaptation fails'' might suggest
and we scope it accordingly: a single test year, 3 pixels, and no repeated-seed runs
(\Cref{sec:limitations}), so we report ``no observed gain under the tested configurations'' rather
than claiming a reproducible non-improvement has been established in the statistical sense.
\Cref{tab:adaptation} summarizes. Original (unvalidated) LoRA fine-tuning is uniformly worse
($\Delta R^2 = -0.163, -0.014, -0.120$ across the 3 pixels). Adding validation-based checkpoint
selection recovers most, but not all, of this gap on the two pixels where it was worst
(\texttt{low\_amplitude}: $-0.042$ remaining; \texttt{evergreen}: $-0.003$, effectively closed)
without ever crossing zero-shot outright. Extending to six further PEFT methods spanning a
1{,}500-fold range of trainable-parameter count (8{,}352 to 13{,}093{,}536) -- additive low-rank
(DoRA), multiplicative rescaling (IA3), bias-only (BitFit), normalization-only (LN-Tuning),
shared-random-projection (VeRA), and full-weight partial fine-tuning -- finds the same pattern:
mean $R^2$ across all fine-tuning variants is uniformly below zero-shot's 0.780, and only one
method/pixel combination (DoRA on \texttt{evergreen}, $+0.0026$) beats zero-shot at all, by a
margin plausibly attributable to noise. Trainable-parameter count is, at best, a weak and
inconsistent predictor of outcome (pooled $r = +0.10$ with test $R^2$; the sign of the correlation
even flips between pixels). We note, following \Cref{sec:results-climatology}, that the zero-shot
reference these methods are measured against does not itself consistently exceed a training-period
climatology baseline; none of the nine adaptation variants here was separately benchmarked against
climatology, so we cannot report whether any of them exceeds that lower bar even where they fall
short of zero-shot. Given the scale of effort an additional adaptation sweep would require, and
that the climatology comparison raises a logically prior question -- does the covariate
information these methods are being asked to exploit carry skill beyond seasonality in the first
place (\Cref{sec:results-climatology})? -- we treat that question, not a further expansion of the
adaptation search, as the more urgent next step (\Cref{sec:discussion}).

The vegetation-composition-conditioning experiments extend this finding with a mechanistic
explanation rather than merely replicating it. A single-pixel, constant PFT covariate is
\textbf{architecturally provably erased} by Chronos-2's per-series InstanceNorm
(\Cref{eq:in-loc}--\Cref{eq:in-scaled}) -- confirmed by direct source inspection and an exact,
8-decimal-place-zero empirical perturbation test. Fixing this with a FiLM-conditioning head
(\Cref{eq:film}) genuinely capable of learning a PFT-dependent response (verified via a smoke test
and a deliberately-overfit checkpoint that shows large, physically plausible sensitivity, up to
0.63 LAI units) still fails to produce a validated improvement: across four architecturally
distinct conditioning designs -- spanning an 8$\times$ range in trainable parameters, with and
without dropout and weight decay -- three of four show validation loss increasing from the first
evaluated checkpoint onward even given 8 rolling training windows (4$\times$ more temporal
supervision than the initial 2-window design). We describe this as \emph{immediate validation
deterioration} rather than diagnosing it as overfitting specifically: we did not inspect training
loss curves or isolate learning rate, initialization, or optimization-target effects as alternative
explanations, so the result establishes that these three designs fail to validate under the tested
protocol, not the specific mechanism by which they fail. The fourth (\texttt{low\_rank}, a
rank-8-bottleneck design) is the only architecture that
trains past step 0 -- and the key result of this control is that an identical \texttt{low\_rank} head
trained on \textbf{randomly shuffled} PFT-to-pixel assignments improves 2022 $R^2$ by $+0.0024$,
marginally \emph{more} than the real-PFT condition's $+0.0022$, with no statistically detectable
difference between them (paired $t$-test $p=0.245$, Wilcoxon $p=0.663$, $n=70$ pixels; 31/70
pixels favor real PFT, 39/70 favor shuffled). Neither seasonal-phase breakdown nor
mixed-vs-pure-pixel entropy analysis reveals any shuffle-surviving signal.

\subsection{Failure cases and challenging environmental conditions}
\label{sec:results-failure}

The \texttt{evergreen}/2012 hindcast failure (zero-shot $R^2 = -7.64$; original LoRA
$R^2 = -8.29$, the single worst score of all 9 methods compared this way) is clarified, though not
conclusively diagnosed, by a leakage experiment: allowing 2012 to participate in LoRA fitting (a
deliberate leakage condition, \textbf{not} a valid evaluation) recovers RMSE by 59.9\% and $R^2$ to
$-0.50$ -- the second-largest recovery of 9 methods tested this way (behind only Random Forest,
$+8.01$), landing squarely in the middle of the baseline pack rather than uniquely bad. We read
this recovery, consistent across essentially every architecture tested (tree ensemble, six neural
sequence models, and a large pretrained transformer), as evidence \emph{consistent with} a shared
external cause -- the 2012 drought being genuinely unlike anything in 2000--2011 training data --
rather than an architecture-specific representational limitation. We stop short of calling this
resolved: exposing 2012 to model fitting simultaneously changes the available climate information
\emph{and} the available LAI information for that year, so the recovery is also consistent with
partial memorization of the test-year target itself, and this diagnostic cannot separate the two
explanations. A cleaner test -- exposing 2012's climate only, with 2012's LAI still held out --
would be needed to attribute the recovery specifically to distribution shift in the covariates
rather than to direct target leakage.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{Three different things are easy to conflate in this evaluation, and we keep them separate
here.} (1) \emph{Seasonal-cycle reconstruction}: how well a model reproduces the predictable
annual LAI trajectory at a given site. This is what a training-period climatology baseline does by
construction, and it is responsible for most of the raw $R^2$ at high-amplitude sites
(\Cref{sec:results-climatology}). (2) \emph{Genuine interannual anomaly skill}: forecasting the
deviation from that predictable cycle in a specific year -- the quantity the ACC metric targets,
and the one most directly relevant to the practical use case of anticipating an unusual season.
This is real but inconsistent for zero-shot Chronos-2 (positive in roughly three-quarters of the
33 hindcast cases, negative in the rest). (3) \emph{The value of known future meteorology
specifically}: Chronos-2's native covariate support lets it condition on actual future climate,
which none of the eight supervised baselines can use at all (\Cref{sec:results-comparison}); this
is a genuine capability advantage, but it is a statement about covariate access, not about the
pretrained representation's forecasting skill per se, and this study does not isolate how much of
Chronos-2's edge over the baselines is attributable to (3) versus to (1) or (2). Treating a result
from one of these three as evidence for another -- for example, reading high raw $R^2$ (driven
largely by (1)) as evidence of strong transferred forecasting skill (which is really about (2)) --
is, we argue, the single most important source of overclaiming risk in applying TSFMs to seasonal
Earth-system variables, and is the reason \Cref{sec:results-climatology} is placed first in the
Results section rather than reported as a supplementary check.

\textbf{What does the climatology-referenced evidence imply, and what doesn't it imply?} It implies
that, for practitioners with access to reliable future meteorological forcing, a general-purpose
TSFM is a credible default that is at least competitive with, and at low-seasonal-signal sites
measurably ahead of, both specialized supervised models and a trivial seasonal baseline -- without
investing in a purpose-built architecture or a target-domain training pipeline. It does not imply
that Chronos-2 has learned a generically superior representation of vegetation--climate dynamics,
nor that its zero-shot skill reliably exceeds climatology: at the site with the strongest seasonal
cycle, it does not. This is a narrower, more qualified claim than ``strong zero-shot transfer''
taken at face value would suggest, and it extends less straightforwardly than it might first
appear to prior evidence that Chronos-2 zero-shot performance is strong relative to fine-tuning on
small datasets \citep{arxiv2607.23146} -- that comparison is zero-shot-vs-fine-tuned on the
\emph{same} model family, not zero-shot-vs-climatology, and the two questions can have different
answers. Separately, this project's comparison with the eight supervised baselines is not
same-task (\Cref{sec:results-comparison}), and the baselines' collapse under spatial transfer (in
sharp contrast to Chronos-2's success, \Cref{sec:results-robustness}) suggests at least some of
them were fitting genuinely useful, if non-transferable, location-specific structure that a
covariate-blind, climatology-blind comparison cannot credit them for.

\textbf{When do supervised models retain advantages?} Random Forest is the single most consistent
performer across the entire rolling-window hindcast study (lowest rank volatility, comparable mean rank to
Chronos-2) and degrades most gracefully of all 10 methods on the one severe drought fold --
evidence that a simple, well-regularized supervised model trained directly on the target series
retains real advantages in anomalous conditions a pretrained zero-shot model has not encountered.

\textbf{Why might fine-tuning fail to improve a strong pretrained model, here specifically?} Three
lines of evidence converge: (1) each fine-tuning run adapts on a single, short ($\sim$1{,}000-point)
series -- the scenario Chronos-2's own documentation identifies as a regime where fine-tuning
``may not improve over zero-shot (and may even worsen accuracy sometimes)''; (2)
trainable-parameter count is a weak, inconsistent predictor of outcome across a 1{,}500$\times$
range, arguing against ``insufficient capacity'' as the explanation; (3) the PFT shuffle control
directly demonstrates that added conditioning capacity produces a similar effect size whether or
not it carries real information, which is difficult to reconcile with any story in which the
\emph{specific} information being added is the limiting factor. Together, these support the
hypothesis that the binding constraint is the \textbf{combination} of a strong pretrained prior
and a small amount of target-domain data, not any one adaptation method's design.

\textbf{What environmental regimes remain difficult?} Sustained, multi-month anomalies genuinely
absent from a model's training window (\Cref{sec:results-failure}) remain hard for every method
tested, Chronos-2 included, and Chronos-2 shows no special resistance to this specific failure
mode -- an important tempering of the paper's otherwise favorable robustness story
(\Cref{sec:results-robustness}).

\textbf{Implications for future vegetation foundation-model research.} The clearest actionable
implication is methodological: any future claim that an adaptation or conditioning method improves
a strong pretrained forecaster on limited data should be checked against a randomized-assignment
control before being reported as a positive result -- this project's own preliminary multi-pixel
PFT-conditioning experiment would have been reported as a promising, if inconclusive, positive
direction without the shuffle control added afterward, and that control reversed the reading
entirely.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small core sample.} The primary cross-model comparison rests on 3 pixels; rank
    volatility among several baselines (e.g., GRU swinging from rank 2 to rank 10) is a direct
    warning that single-digit-pixel-count rankings, including ``Chronos-2 is best,'' could shift
    with more locations. Three additional pre-selected pixels exist but have not been run through
    Chronos-2.
  \item \textbf{Task asymmetry.} Chronos-2's headline comparison against the baselines is not
    same-information; the baselines never receive future climate, or (\Cref{sec:methods-baselines})
    their own target's historical values. This is repeatedly flagged in-text
    (\Cref{sec:results-comparison}) and should not be read as a pure architecture verdict; a
    matched-information baseline and a Chronos-2 ablation without future covariates, neither of
    which this study runs, would be needed to decompose the gap into its future-covariate,
    historical-target, and pretraining-specific components.
  \item \textbf{The climatology baseline is simple by design, and 3 sites is a small sample for
    it too.} \Cref{sec:results-climatology}'s day-of-year climatology has no trend term, no
    explicit drought/anomaly adjustment, and is recomputed independently per fold; a more
    sophisticated classical baseline (e.g.\ harmonic regression with a trend term, or a seasonal
    ARIMA) might close or widen the gap with Chronos-2 differently than a bare climatology does.
    The climatology comparison is computed on the same 3 core sites as the rest of this paper, so
    the small-sample caveat above applies to it equally -- a wider climatology-referenced
    evaluation, ideally on the 70-site pooled set, is the most direct way to test whether the
    site-dependent pattern reported here (added skill at low amplitude, none detected at high
    amplitude) generalizes.
  \item \textbf{Geographic scope.} All experiments are CONUS-only (HiQ-LAI/gridMET coverage); a
    global ERA5-based generalization study is underway but has produced no results as of this
    writing.
  \item \textbf{Single pretrained checkpoint, unseeded fine-tuning stochasticity.} All results use
    one Chronos-2 checkpoint (\texttt{amazon/chronos-2}); the fine-tuning dataset's
    training-window sampling is not seeded across runs, introducing run-to-run noise on the order
    of the leakage diagnostic's $\sim$0.6 $\Delta R^2$ reproduction gap between two nominally
    identical fine-tuning conditions.
  \item \textbf{PFT screening is not exhaustive, and several protocol details are not fully
    specified in this draft.} Four conditioning architectures and two learning rates each were
    tested; conditioning additional (covariate) rows beyond the target row, and
    cross-attention/mixture-of-experts-style gating, were reasoned about but not run. The
    current draft does not state, and should before submission: whether the 70-pixel PFT
    experiment's train and test sets share pixels or are spatially disjoint; the number of random
    seeds and the number of distinct shuffled-assignment draws underlying the shuffle-control
    result (\Cref{sec:results-adaptation}); and the PFT product, reference year, and resampling
    method behind the composition vectors themselves. We note this explicitly rather than guessing
    at values not independently confirmed in the materials available while preparing this draft.
  \item \textbf{MAPE's sample varies by method.} \Cref{eq:mape} excludes any time step where either
    the true or predicted value is non-positive, so the set of steps contributing to a model's MAPE
    is not identical across methods with different numbers of non-positive predictions; RMSE, MAE,
    and $R^2$, which do not exclude any steps, are the metrics on which cross-model comparisons in
    this paper should be primarily read.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper set out to answer a more specific question than whether a general-purpose time-series
foundation model can forecast LAI: how much skill does it add beyond a simple, interpretable
seasonal climatology baseline, and where does that skill hold up or break down? Across a
systematic evaluation spanning the climatology-referenced hindcast (33 paired cases), a single-year
cross-model comparison, 330 model--case evaluations across the 33-case rolling-window hindcast, a long-distance spatial
transfer test, and the most extensive adaptation study conducted in this project -- seven
parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods and four vegetation-composition-conditioning architectures,
including a randomized control -- the answer is site-dependent and more qualified than a
headline raw-accuracy comparison alone would suggest. Paired against climatology across all 33
hindcast cases, zero-shot Chronos-2 shows no statistically detectable advantage, and at the site
responsible for this study's highest raw $R^2$ values, climatology's mean $R^2$ is \emph{higher}.
At the lowest-amplitude site, where the seasonal signal is weakest, Chronos-2's advantage over
climatology is real and positive, and its anomaly-correlation skill is positive, though
inconsistent, across roughly three-quarters of all 33 hindcast cases. Zero-shot Chronos-2 also
remains competitive with eight specialized supervised baselines under an information regime that
favors it (actual future climate, which the baselines cannot use at all), shows no observed
degradation in a single 3{,}700\,km spatial-transfer test (one source--target pair, no
uncertainty estimate), and -- with one important, honestly-reported
exception (sustained climate anomalies genuinely absent from its context) -- degrades gracefully
across most of the years tested. None of eleven distinct adaptation mechanisms tested shows a
consistent gain over this zero-shot reference -- one individual method--pixel combination (DoRA at
\texttt{evergreen}) and the PFT-conditioning head both show small positive margins that do not
generalize across pixels or survive a randomized control, respectively -- and the project's
decisive randomized control shows that even the one PFT-conditioning architecture that trains
successfully derives no detectable benefit from real vegetation information over randomly shuffled
input -- though, as this paper also argues,
that zero-shot reference is itself not consistently better than a model with no learned
representation of climate--vegetation dynamics at all, which qualifies how much the adaptation
results should be read as evidence about the pretrained representation's ceiling specifically,
versus about the limited headroom available over climatology on this dataset more broadly. For
vegetation LAI forecasting, and plausibly for other seasonal, data-limited Earth-system tasks with
structurally similar covariates, the actionable conclusion is narrower than ``deploy general-purpose
TSFMs zero-shot as a strong default'': deploy them where a climatology-referenced evaluation shows
them earning their complexity, report that comparison alongside raw accuracy, and treat
high raw $R^2$ on a strongly seasonal target as a question about seasonal-cycle reconstruction
until a baseline this cheap to compute has been ruled out as the explanation.
